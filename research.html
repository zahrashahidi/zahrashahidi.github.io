Spatial Information Frictions in Rural-Urban Migration

Draft

with Tasso Adamopoulos

Abstract

We study the role of information frictions for the low and slow out-migration of workers from rural to urban areas in developing countries. Using panel data from Indonesia we document that migration flows are highly persistent across space and that current district-level migration is correlated with incomes of previous migrants from the same districts. Using a difference-in-differences design and exploiting natural disaster events at origin as push-shocks, we show that rural districts with improved internet access exhibit higher migration to urban areas. We develop a quantitative spatial model of imperfect information with noisy and dispersed information across rural districts about urban earnings opportunities. Our theory features localized social learning, where rural residents learn about urban opportunities from previous migrants from their own districts. We estimate the model to the panel data from Indonesia and identify information frictions using indirect inference, with easily computable second moments in micro-level datasets. We allow for other migration frictions to disentangle the role of information per se. We find that our theory can account for low aggregate migration and the spatial persistence of migration over time. Information frictions account for about 1/5 of total migration costs and generate a welfare loss of 2.47 percent for rural workers. We validate the learning mechanism and the model’s quantitative predictions using elicited beliefs from experimental evidence in Kenya.

Misbelief and Education Decisions by Gender

Slides

How much of the gender gap in college completion can be explained by differences in beliefs?

Research overview

Women now earn more bachelor’s degrees than men across OECD countries, while men are more likely to leave college without completing a degree. This paper studies whether gender differences in subjective beliefs about graduation help explain that pattern. Using the National Longitudinal Survey of Youth 1997, I compare individuals’ expected chances of earning a four-year degree with their later enrollment and graduation outcomes.

I find that both women and men overestimate their graduation chances, but men’s beliefs are noisier signals of academic skill. Men also respond more strongly to those less-informative beliefs when making education decisions. I embed these facts in a general-equilibrium education-choice model in which subjective beliefs determine enrollment while skill and effort determine graduation. The framework shows how gendered misbeliefs can generate over-enrollment, dropout, and the misallocation of talent, and provides a basis for evaluating belief-correction policies and their wage and welfare effects.

Misallocation of Female Talent: The Puzzling Case of Iran

Draft / Slides

Analyzing how barriers reduce female labor force participation and distort the allocation of talent, with consequences for aggregate economic outcomes.

Abstract

This paper addresses talent misallocation in Iran, focusing on the historical underrepresentation of women in the market and the gender-based disparities in sectoral distribution. I develop a simple multi-sector general equilibrium model that incorporates barriers to labor force participation and occupational choices by gender. The findings of this paper reveal that in 1990, barriers to labor force participation for females were higher than for males. Fast forward to 2020, these barriers had significantly decreased, with women experiencing more substantial gains. These reductions led to a 4% increase in the participation rate and a threefold surge in female employment within high-skill occupations. The study also reveals that in all sectors, the decrease in barriers had a more significant impact on females than males. Furthermore, the friction decomposition indicates that, for females, these fixed cost reductions were more due to the decrease in barriers rather than productivity improvements. These findings highlight a distinct pattern in reducing labor market friction between genders and suggest a significant misallocation of female talent in Iran.
